The problem I found

I started by checking the network before trying to optimize it. The data describes historical orders alongside current warehouse and shipping restrictions. PLANT03 handled 8,541 historical orders, but its current capacity is only 7,091 orders over seven days.

Reassignment helps, but eligibility rules limit the choices: 7,043 orders must still use PLANT03. This leaves just 48 slots at that plant across the seven-day scenario.

What that means

Unused capacity elsewhere cannot solve the bottleneck unless those warehouses can supply the product, serve the customer and use a valid shipping route.

Only 1,653 orders have more than one eligible warehouse. Orders with a required warehouse can still have different route and shipping-day choices.

This is a planning test under current constraints, not evidence that historical orders were late.

From Excel checks to Python automation

  1. Check the rules on an example.I used Excel and Power Query to join product support, customer restrictions and plant-to-port links. For product 1689547 in the PLANT03 example, the checks found 192 eligible orders at PLANT03, 89 at PLANT02 and 9 at PLANT10.
  2. Repeat the workflow for every order.Python reproduced the Excel findings and applied the same checks to all 9,215 orders. Capacity was measured in orders, not product units.
  3. Choose warehouses, routes and days together.I used mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) because orders stay whole, warehouses share daily capacity, and compatible orders can share freight pricing groups.
  4. Make the search practical.The first formulation ran for over six hours without returning a complete plan. I removed impossible or always-worse options, calculated fixed prices once and supplied a checked starting schedule. The final search could still change warehouses, routes and days.
  5. Check the saved result.Every order was assigned once. Product, customer, port, service, weight-band and daily capacity checks passed, and the exported costs reconciled.

Obstacles that changed the approach

Missing historical freight matches

Historical carriers and weight bands did not always match the current rate card. I could compare warehouse costs using the same rates, but could not establish a complete historical freight baseline. The project therefore reports scenario freight separately and does not claim total historical shipping savings.

Excel joins became too large to review

Joining freight bands produced about a million rows. I kept Excel for examples and summaries, then used Python to repeat the workflow across the full dataset. I also repaired a missing-column query step and preserved the original order IDs.

A large model did not return a plan

More than six hours of searching produced a lower bound but no complete assignment. A validated starting schedule and a smaller equivalent pricing formulation made the final search practical without permanently fixing shipping days or reducing the order sample.

Other projects reported different totals

I checked differences in order coverage, freight pricing and capacity assumptions. A lower published number is not directly comparable unless it solves the same problem under the same rules.

Scope and assumptions

  • This is a public-data portfolio case, not a client engagement or evidence of customer complaints.
  • All orders are available at the start of a seven-day scenario. Processing and dispatch use the same assigned day; delivery deadlines and delay penalties are not modeled.
  • Warehouse cost is quantity multiplied by unit cost, rather than a daily holding charge.
  • Compatible orders on the same route and day share a freight weight band. AIR pricing applies per order; GROUND pricing applies to the group. Customer-arranged freight has zero company freight cost.
  • The 48 spare PLANT03 slots show limited capacity headroom. A capacity-loss stress test has not been completed.

Source: Brunel Supply Chain Logistics Problem Dataset. Results shown are from one saved and checked run.
