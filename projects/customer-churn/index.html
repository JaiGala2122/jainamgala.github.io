Customer Churn Prediction

20.4% of 10,000 European bank customers left. Gradient boosting ranks who is likely to leave. Contacting the riskiest 20% reaches 63% of the people who churn.

  • 20.4% left, 2,037 of 10,000
  • 0.870 ROC-AUC, gradient boosting
  • 63% of churners in the riskiest 20%
  • 0.3 cutoff, 64.6% of churners caught

How many customers leave

2,037 of 10,000 customers left, which is 20.4%. About 1 in 5. A model that predicts “everyone stays” would already be 79.6% accurate, so accuracy is not the headline metric.

Segment rates below use all 10,000 customers. Model scores use the 2,000-customer test set, which contains 407 churners.

Who leaves

Age is the biggest driver, and churn peaks between 46 and 60

Churn climbs from age 35, peaks at 58.4% for ages 51–55, then falls. Customers over 65 churn at 13.3%, below the average. A single “50+” rate of 44.6% hides that drop.

Customers with 3–4 products almost all leave

Two products is the low point, at 7.6%. Customers with 3 or 4 products churn at 82.7% and 100%. That group is small: 326 customers, 3.3% of the book.

Germany and inactive members churn the most

German customers churn at 32.4%, about twice France (16.2%) and Spain (16.7%). Inactive members churn at 26.9%, against 14.3% for active members. Inactive customers in Germany are the highest cell, at 41.1%.

Gender gap

Women churn at 25.1% and men at 16.5%, a gap of 8.6 points. Women over 50 churn at 52.8% (604 customers).

What does not matter

Tenure does not separate leavers from stayers. Churn stays between 17% and 23% at every tenure from 0 to 10 years. A credit card does not either: 20.8% without one, 20.2% with one.

Model results

Gradient boosting was selected because it had the highest ROC-AUC. ROC-AUC measures how well the model ranks customers by risk. 0.5 is random guessing and 1.0 is perfect. It is not the share of churners the model catches.

What the model uses

Age and number of products account for most of the ranking. Feature importance shows what the model relies on. It does not show what causes churn.

Choosing the cutoff

At the default 0.5 cutoff the model catches 48.6% of churners. A lower cutoff catches more of them and flags more people who would have stayed. At 0.3, recall and precision are both about 64%.

How well the ranking concentrates churners

Contacting the riskiest 20% of customers reaches 63.1% of all churners, 3.2 times a random list. In the top 10%, 84.5% of the customers contacted actually leave.

Recommendations

  1. Score every customer monthly and contact the riskiest 20% first. That list reaches 63% of the customers who are about to leave.
  2. Use a 0.3 probability cutoff for the “at risk” flag, not 0.5. It catches 64.6% of churners instead of 48.6%, and about 6 in 10 flagged customers actually leave.
  3. Focus retention on customers aged 46–60. Their churn rate is 46–58%, more than double the average. Customers over 65 churn at only 13.3%.
  4. Win back inactive German customers. They churn at 41.1%. Usage rewards and check-in calls fit here, because inactivity is something the bank can influence.
  5. Investigate customers with 3–4 products. 83–100% of them leave, in a small group of 326 people. Moving 1-product customers to 2 products is the safer offer, at 7.6% churn.
  6. Look into the gender gap. Women churn at 25.1% against 16.5% for men, and at 52.8% over age 50.
  7. Retrain the model each quarter and check that recall at the 0.3 cutoff stays around 65% on new data.

Limits

  • Results come from one dataset of 10,000 European customers and a single 80/20 split. They have not been tested on newer data.
  • The 3- and 4-product groups are 326 customers, so those churn rates are a signal, not a precise figure.
  • Feature importance shows what the model relies on, not what causes a customer to leave.
  • The gradient boosting ROC curve on this page uses the approximate points from the results note, not a fresh export of every threshold.
